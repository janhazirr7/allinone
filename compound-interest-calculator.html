How to Use the Compound Interest Calculator

Compound interest is the reason small, regular savings can grow into large sums over time: you earn interest not only on your original money, but also on the interest you have already earned. This calculator shows exactly how that snowball effect works for your own numbers, year by year, with no sign-up and no data leaving your browser.

  1. Enter your principal amountEnter the starting sum, for example 10000 if you are investing ten thousand dollars.
  2. Enter the annual interest rateType the yearly rate as a percentage, for example 5 for five percent. Leave it at 0 to see a no-growth baseline.
  3. Enter the number of yearsChoose how long the money stays invested, from 1 to 50 years. Longer periods show the compounding effect much more clearly.
  4. Pick a compounding frequency and calculateChoose yearly, quarterly, monthly, or daily compounding, then press Calculate Growth to see the final amount, the interest earned, and the full year-by-year table.

The Compound Interest Formula Explained

Banks and investment accounts do not simply add the same interest every year. After each compounding period, the interest is added to the balance, so the next period earns interest on a slightly larger amount. That repeated process is what makes the growth curve bend upward over time instead of rising in a straight line.

A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n × t)
Where:
• P = principal, the starting amount
• r = annual interest rate as a decimal (5% = 0.05)
• n = number of compounding periods per year (1, 4, 12, or 365)
• t = time in years
• A = final amount
• Interest earned = A − P

Notice what the formula rewards most: time. Doubling the interest rate roughly doubles the yearly gain, but adding ten extra years can multiply the final amount, because each new year compounds on everything that came before it. That is why financial advisers repeat the same advice so often: start early, even with a small amount, and let time do the heavy lifting.

Worked Example: $10,000 at 5% for 10 Years

Take a principal of $10,000, an annual rate of 5%, and a ten-year horizon. With yearly compounding, the calculation is $10,000 × (1.05)^10, which equals $16,288.95. The interest earned is therefore $6,288.95, which is more than sixty percent on top of the original deposit, even though the yearly rate was only five percent. Switch the frequency to monthly compounding and the same inputs give $16,470.09, because interest is added twelve times a year instead of once. Daily compounding pushes the result to about $16,487. The differences between frequencies are real but modest; the far bigger levers are the rate and, above all, the number of years.

Compare that with simple interest, where the same $10,000 at 5% for 10 years would earn exactly $5,000, for a total of $15,000. Compounding adds almost $1,289 extra in this example, and the gap keeps widening every year because the compounded balance grows faster than a straight line. Over thirty years the difference becomes dramatic, which is why retirement savings and long-term deposits rely on compounding.

1. Start early, even small

Money invested at age 25 has ten more compounding years than money invested at 35. Those extra years often matter more than investing a larger sum later, so a modest early start usually beats a bigger late start.

2. Leave the interest alone

Withdrawing interest each year turns a compound account into a simple-interest account. Reinvesting every dollar of interest keeps the snowball rolling and is the single easiest way to increase the final amount.

3. Watch inflation and fees

A 5% return is worth less if prices rise 3% a year, and account fees quietly reduce the balance that compounds. Always compare the rate after fees and against inflation, not just the headline percentage.

A final caution: this calculator assumes a fixed rate, no deposits or withdrawals during the period, and no taxes. Real accounts vary, so treat the result as a clear planning estimate rather than a guaranteed promise, and check the exact terms of any bank or investment product before committing money.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

What is compound interest?
Compound interest is interest earned on both the original principal and on interest earned in previous periods. Each time interest is added to the balance, the next period earns interest on that larger balance, so growth accelerates over time instead of staying flat.
How is compound interest different from simple interest?
Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal, so it grows by the same amount every year. Compound interest is calculated on the growing balance, so the amount of interest earned increases each period. Over long periods, compounding produces a much larger final amount.
Does compounding frequency really matter?
Yes, but less than time and rate. Moving from yearly to monthly compounding on $10,000 at 5% for 10 years raises the final amount from $16,288.95 to $16,470.09. Daily compounding adds only a little more. Frequency matters, but starting early and choosing a good rate matter far more.
What happens if the interest rate is 0%?
If the rate is 0%, no interest is earned and the final amount equals the principal, whatever the compounding frequency. The calculator handles this correctly: the interest earned shows as $0.00 and the year-by-year table stays flat.
Is my financial data saved or sent anywhere?
No. OmniTools runs entirely in your browser. Your principal, rate, and years are never sent to a server, stored in a database, or shared with anyone.
