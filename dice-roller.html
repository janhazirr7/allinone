    How to Use the Dice Roller

    Whether you are playing a board game, running a tabletop role-playing session, or teaching probability, you sometimes need dice you do not have on hand, or more dice than fit in your palm. This roller covers the standard polyhedral dice used in modern games: d4, d6, d8, d10, d12, d20, and d100. Choose how many dice to roll, pick the die type, and press Roll Dice to see every individual result, the total, and a history of your recent rolls.

    1. Choose the number of diceRoll from 1 to 10 dice at once. Rolling 3d6 for a character's strength, or 2d20 with advantage in mind, is a single click instead of three separate rolls.
    2. Pick the die typeSelect the number of sides: 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 20, or 100. The classic six-sided die suits board games, while the twenty-sided die is the heart of most role-playing games.
    3. Roll and read the resultEach die shows its own face value, so you can see exactly which dice rolled high or low, and the total is highlighted at the top. The possible range, from all ones to all maximums, is shown for reference.
    4. Check the historyThe last 10 rolls stay listed below the result, so you can settle disputes, track a run of luck, or simply remember what you rolled two turns ago.

    Dice Notation and What to Expect on Average

    Gamers write dice rolls in a compact notation: the number before the letter d is how many dice to roll, and the number after it is how many sides each die has. So 2d6 means roll two six-sided dice and add them, 1d20 means a single twenty-sided die, and 3d8 means three eight-sided dice. This roller follows that convention exactly, and its history labels each roll the same way, such as 2d6, so the record is easy to read at a glance.

    Every fair die has an average value right in the middle of its range. For a single die, the expected value is the number of sides plus one, divided by two: a d6 averages 3.5, a d20 averages 10.5, and a d100 averages 50.5. Multiply by the number of dice for the average total, so 2d6 averages 7 and 3d6 averages 10.5. That average is a useful anchor, but individual rolls swing widely around it. With 2d6, a total of 7 is the most common result because six different combinations produce it, while 2 and 12 each have only one combination. Understanding that bell shape, where middle totals are common and extremes are rare, is the beginning of real probability intuition, and it is why game designers choose particular dice for particular jobs.

    Fairness, Streaks, and Good Table Manners

    Digital dice use the same browser random generator as the random number tool, giving every face an equal chance on every roll. That fairness is easy to state and surprisingly hard to feel: humans expect randomness to look evenly spread, so a run of three low rolls feels suspicious even though it is perfectly normal. With a d20, the chance of any single face is 1 in 20, and short streaks happen all the time across a long evening of play. If a result matters for a whole group, roll where everyone can see the screen, agree the die type and count before rolling, and let the history list settle any disagreement about what was rolled.

    1. Advantage and disadvantage

    Some games ask you to roll two d20s and keep the higher (advantage) or lower (disadvantage) one. Roll 2d20 here, look at the individual dice, and keep the one your rules require.

    2. Damage and healing

    Weapon damage like 2d6 or spell effects like 4d8 are one click here, with every die visible so the table can check the maths instead of trusting a hidden total.

    3. Teaching probability

    Roll 2d6 fifty times and tally the totals. The pile around 7, with 2 and 12 barely appearing, teaches the bell curve better than any textbook diagram.

    Keep the limitations in mind too. This roller is for games, teaching, and casual decisions, not for gambling or any contest where money or prizes depend on an audited result. For those, use certified equipment or systems. For a game night, a classroom, or a quick decision among friends, it is fast, free, and private: every roll happens in your browser, nothing is sent to a server, and the last 10 rolls are always there when you need to look back.

    Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

    What does 2d6 mean?
    Dice notation puts the number of dice before the letter d and the number of sides after it. 2d6 means roll two six-sided dice and add them together. 1d20 means one twenty-sided die, and 3d8 means three eight-sided dice.
    What is the average roll of a die?
    For a single fair die, the average is the number of sides plus one, divided by two. A d6 averages 3.5, a d20 averages 10.5, and a d100 averages 50.5. For several dice, multiply by the count: 2d6 averages 7.
    Why is 7 the most common result of 2d6?
    Because six different combinations add up to 7 (1+6, 2+5, 3+4, 4+3, 5+2, 6+1), while 2 and 12 each have only one combination. Middle totals are always more common than extremes when you add dice together.
    Are digital dice rolls fair?
    Yes, for games and teaching. Every face has an equal chance on every roll, using your browser's random generator. Short streaks of high or low rolls are normal in random data and do not mean the roller is biased.
    How many rolls does the history keep?
    The last 10 rolls. Older rolls drop off the list automatically as you keep rolling, so the history stays short and readable.
