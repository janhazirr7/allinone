How to use the water intake calculator

Water keeps almost every system in your body working: it carries nutrients around, regulates temperature through sweat, cushions joints, and helps your kidneys remove waste. Yet the familiar advice to drink eight glasses a day was never personalised, because a small sedentary person and a large athlete in hot weather plainly do not need the same amount. This calculator starts from your body weight, which is the strongest simple predictor of fluid needs, and then adds extra water for exercise and heat, the two factors that most increase sweat losses on a normal day.

  1. Enter your body weightType your current weight and choose kilograms or pounds. The calculator converts pounds to kilograms before it calculates anything.
  2. Choose your activity levelSedentary means little movement beyond daily life. Moderate means regular exercise. Active means intense training or a physical job, where you sweat noticeably most days.
  3. Choose your climatePick hot if you live or work in high heat, or if you are sweating heavily for another reason. Otherwise leave it on normal or mild.
  4. Read litres and glassesThe headline number is your total for the day in litres. The glasses figure divides that total by 250 millilitres, the size of a standard drinking glass, so you can count easily through the day.

How the calculation works

This calculator uses a widely taught rule of thumb: about 33 millilitres of water per kilogram of body weight per day as a base. That is 0.033 litres per kilogram, so a 70 kilogram adult starts at 2.31 litres. It is a middle estimate between the common 30 and 35 millilitre figures used in nutrition teaching, and it is deliberately simple so that anyone can check it by hand. On top of that base, the calculator adds half a litre for moderate activity, a full litre for active days, and half a litre for hot conditions. Those additions are allowances for sweat, not precise measurements of your sweat rate, which varies a great deal between people.

Daily water (L) = weight (kg) x 0.033 + activity extra + climate extra
Example: 70 kg, moderate activity, hot climate: 70 x 0.033 = 2.31 L base + 0.50 L activity + 0.50 L heat = 3.31 L per day, which is about 13 glasses of 250 ml. The same person on a sedentary day in mild weather needs 2.31 L, or about 9 glasses.

Remember that this total is your fluid target from all drinks, and food counts too. Fruits, vegetables, soups, and other moist foods typically provide around a fifth of daily water for people who eat a varied diet. If you eat a lot of fresh food, you may need slightly less from drinks alone, while a very dry diet pushes you the other way. Coffee and tea also count toward fluid intake in normal amounts, despite the old myth that they dehydrate you; the diuretic effect is small when you drink them regularly.

Signs, timing, and common mistakes

Thirst is a useful guide for most healthy adults, but it lags behind actual need during exercise and heat, so waiting until you are very thirsty is not the best strategy on active days. Pale yellow urine is a simple everyday check: very dark urine often means you need more fluid, while completely colourless urine over a long period can mean you are drinking more than you need. Headaches, tiredness, dry mouth, and poor concentration can all be early signs of mild dehydration, although each of those symptoms has many other causes, so they are hints rather than proof.

One mistake is treating the result as a minimum to force down even when you are not thirsty and your urine is already pale. Drinking far beyond your needs can dilute blood sodium, a rare but serious condition called hyponatraemia, which is most often seen in endurance athletes who drink very large volumes during long events. The opposite mistake is counting only plain water and ignoring everything else you drink, which makes the target look harder than it is. Keep the target in perspective: it is a guide for a normal healthy day, not a medical prescription.

Practical ways to reach your target

The easiest method is to anchor drinks to things you already do: a glass when you wake up, one with each meal, one mid-morning, one mid-afternoon, and one in the evening will already cover five glasses for most people. A reusable bottle marked with times, or simply refilling the same bottle a known number of times, turns a vague goal into something countable. If you dislike plain water, sparkling water, herbal tea, and water with lemon or cucumber all count, and so does milk in moderation as part of your normal diet.

1. Front-load the morning

Many people drink too little before lunch and then try to catch up at night, which disturbs sleep. Aim for about a third of your target by midday.

2. Keep water visible

A bottle on your desk is a reminder you cannot miss. A bottle in another room is a plan you have to remember, and most days you will not.

3. Adjust, do not obsess

On a rest day in cool weather you need less than on a training day in heat. Use this calculator for each kind of day and let the number move with your life.

This calculator is for healthy adults and general education only. People with kidney disease, heart failure, liver disease, or conditions that limit fluid intake, and anyone taking diuretics or other medicines that affect fluid balance, should follow their clinician's fluid limit even if it differs from this calculator. Pregnant and breastfeeding people also have higher fluid needs and should use professional guidance rather than a generic figure.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

How much water should I drink per day?
It depends mainly on your weight, activity, and climate. As a general guide, this calculator starts at 33 ml per kilogram of body weight per day and adds water for exercise and heat, so a 70 kg adult needs about 2.31 litres on a sedentary mild day and about 3.31 litres on a moderate, hot day.
Does food count toward my water intake?
Yes. Fruits, vegetables, soups, and other moist foods typically provide around a fifth of daily water. Coffee and tea in normal amounts also count toward fluid intake, so the calculator total is for all drinks, not only plain water.
Is eight glasses a day the right target for everyone?
No. Eight glasses of 250 ml is 2 litres, which is close for some adults but too little for a large active person in heat and more than a small sedentary person needs. A weight-based estimate is a better starting point than one fixed number for everyone.
Can I drink too much water?
Yes, though it is uncommon. Drinking far more than you need, especially very large volumes during long endurance exercise, can dilute blood sodium. Pale yellow urine and normal thirst are better guides than forcing down a fixed amount regardless of how you feel.
Is my data stored anywhere?
No. This calculator runs entirely in your browser. Your weight, activity level, and climate choice are never sent to a server or stored by OmniTools.
