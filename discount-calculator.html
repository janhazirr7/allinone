How to Use the OmniTools Discount Calculator

Sale signs are designed to make you feel like you are getting a bargain, but the number on the tag is rarely the number you actually pay. Discounts, coupons, and sales tax all change the final total, and doing the arithmetic in your head while standing in a shop is easy to get wrong. This calculator removes the guesswork: type in three numbers and it shows you exactly how much you save, the price after the discount, the tax on that discounted price, and the total you will pay at checkout.

  1. Enter the Original Price Type the full price before any discount, for example 200 for a jacket priced at $200. This field is required and must be greater than zero.
  2. Enter the Discount Percentage Type just the number, without the percent sign — for 25% off, enter 25. The discount must be between 0 and 100. A discount above 100% is impossible, because a shop cannot take more than the full price off, so the calculator will show a friendly error if you enter one.
  3. Enter the Sales Tax Percentage (Optional) If your purchase is taxed, enter the local tax rate, for example 10 for 10% tax. You may leave this field blank, which is treated as 0% tax. Remember that the tax is calculated on the discounted price, not the original price, which is explained in detail below.
  4. Click "Calculate Discount" Instantly see the amount saved, the price after discount, the tax amount, and the final total. Use "Copy Summary" to copy the breakdown, or "Reset" to clear the form and start a new calculation.

The Discount Formula, Step by Step

Every discount calculation follows the same three simple steps. Once you know them, you can check any sale price with a pocket calculator, and you will never be fooled by a misleading "was" price again.

Amount Saved = Original Price × Discount % ÷ 100
Final Price After Discount = Original Price − Amount Saved
Tax Amount = Final Price After Discount × Tax % ÷ 100
Total You Pay = Final Price After Discount + Tax Amount

You can also get the discounted price in one step:
Final Price After Discount = Original Price × (100 − Discount %) ÷ 100

Worked example: A pair of shoes costs $200 and is reduced by 25%. First, the amount saved is $200 × 25 ÷ 100 = $50. The price after discount is therefore $200 − $50 = $150. If sales tax is 10%, the tax is calculated on the discounted $150, not on the original $200, so the tax is $150 × 10 ÷ 100 = $15. The total you pay at checkout is $150 + $15 = $165. Notice that you save $50 on the price, and you also pay $5 less tax than you would have paid on the full price, because the tax base itself has fallen.

Stacked Discounts: Why 20% Plus 10% Is Not 30%

Shops often offer an extra discount on top of a sale price, for example "an extra 10% off already reduced items" or a coupon applied at checkout. It is tempting to add the two percentages together, but that overstates the saving, because the second discount applies only to the already reduced price, not to the original price. This is the single most common discount mistake, and retailers rely on shoppers making it.

Take a $100 item with 20% off, plus an extra 10% off. The first discount takes $20 off, leaving $80. The second discount takes 10% of $80, which is $8, leaving a final price of $72. Your total saving is $28, which is 28%, not 30%. The gap grows with larger discounts: 50% off plus 50% off does not make an item free — it leaves you paying 25% of the original price, because half of a half is a quarter. To use this calculator for a stacked offer, run the first discount, then enter the result as the new original price and apply the second discount.

Sales Tax Explained: Tax Is Charged on the Discounted Price

In most countries and states, sales tax or value-added tax is charged on the amount you actually pay for the item, which is the price after any discount. That means a sale saves you twice: once on the price itself, and again on the tax, because the tax is calculated on a smaller amount. This calculator follows that rule, and it states it clearly in the results so there is no confusion about which price the tax was applied to.

There are a few exceptions worth knowing. In some places, tax is charged before a manufacturer's rebate is applied, because a rebate is treated as a refund after the sale rather than a price reduction at the till. Coupons issued by the shop itself, on the other hand, normally reduce the taxable price. If you are unsure which rule applies to a particular offer, check the receipt: it will show the discounted subtotal and then the tax line beneath it. If the tax was calculated on the higher original price, the receipt will make that obvious, and you can question it.

A related trap is the price shown on the shelf. In the United States, shelf prices usually exclude sales tax, so the checkout total is higher than the tag price. In much of Europe, the displayed price already includes value-added tax. Either way, this calculator lets you model the real checkout total: leave the tax field blank when tax is already included in the price, or enter your local rate when it is added at the till.

Worked Shopping Examples

The best way to build confidence with discounts is to work through real shopping situations. Try each of these in the calculator above and check that you get the same answers.

1. The 25% Off Jacket — $200, No Tax

Original price $200, discount 25%, tax left blank. Amount saved: $200 × 25 ÷ 100 = $50. Price after discount: $150. Tax: $0. Total you pay: $150. You save $50.

2. The Same Jacket With 10% Tax

Original price $200, discount 25%, tax 10%. Amount saved is still $50 and the discounted price is still $150. Tax on the discounted price: $150 × 10 ÷ 100 = $15. Total you pay: $165. Note that the tax is $15, not $20 — tax on the original $200 would have been $20.

3. A $59.99 Item at 40% Off

Original price $59.99, discount 40%, tax 8%. Amount saved: $59.99 × 40 ÷ 100 = $24.00 (rounded). Price after discount: $35.99. Tax: $35.99 × 8 ÷ 100 = $2.88. Total you pay: $38.87. Even with cents in the price, the same three steps give the exact checkout total.

A final shopping tip: always compare the final price, not the size of the discount. A 50% discount on an overpriced item can still cost more than a 20% discount on a fairly priced one. Enter both final prices in this calculator, compare the totals including tax, and let the numbers — not the size of the sale sign — decide which deal is genuinely better.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

How do you calculate a discount price?
Multiply the original price by the discount percentage and divide by 100 to get the amount saved, then subtract that amount from the original price. For example, a $200 item at 25% off saves $50, so the final price is $150.
Is sales tax calculated before or after the discount?
In most places, sales tax is calculated on the discounted price, not the original price. That is how this calculator works: it first subtracts the discount, then applies the tax percentage to the lower, discounted price, so you pay less tax when an item is on sale.
How do stacked discounts work? Is 20% off plus 10% off the same as 30% off?
No. Stacked discounts are applied one after another, not added together. A $100 item at 20% off costs $80, and a further 10% off that $80 saves another $8, giving a final price of $72. That is a total saving of 28%, not 30%. To combine two discounts, enter the first result as the new original price and apply the second discount.
How do I find the discount percentage from two prices?
Subtract the sale price from the original price, divide the result by the original price, and multiply by 100. For example, an item reduced from $80 to $60 saves $20, and $20 divided by $80 times 100 is 25%, so the discount is 25% off.
Is my financial data saved or transmitted to your servers?
Never. OmniTools operates entirely client-side within your device's web browser. No prices, discount percentages, or tax figures are ever sent to external databases or recorded on remote servers.
