    How to use the sleep calculator

    Sleep does not happen in one smooth block. It runs in cycles of roughly ninety minutes, moving from light sleep into deep sleep and then into dreaming sleep, before starting again. Waking up at the end of a cycle, when sleep is lightest, usually feels much easier than being dragged out of deep sleep in the middle of a cycle, even if the total time asleep is slightly shorter. This calculator works backwards or forwards from the time you give it, in whole ninety-minute cycles, and it adds about fifteen minutes for falling asleep, because most people do not fall asleep the instant their head touches the pillow.

    1. Choose a modePick wake-up mode if you must get up at a fixed time, for work or school. Pick bedtime mode if you know when you will go to bed and want the best times to set your alarm.
    2. Enter your timeUse the time picker to enter your wake-up time or bedtime in 24-hour format. The calculator handles times that cross midnight automatically.
    3. Compare four, five, and six cyclesFour cycles is six hours of sleep, five is seven and a half hours, and six is nine hours. Most adults need seven to nine hours, so five or six cycles is the usual target.
    4. Pick the longest option you can actually keepThe best bedtime is the one that gives you the most complete cycles and that you can repeat most nights, not a perfect time you only manage once a week.

    How the calculation works

    The maths is deliberately simple and transparent. For a wake-up time, the calculator subtracts fifteen minutes for falling asleep, then subtracts ninety minutes per cycle. For a bedtime, it adds the same fifteen minutes and then ninety minutes per cycle. The fifteen-minute falling-asleep allowance is an average, not a personal measurement: some people fall asleep in five minutes and others need thirty, so if you know your own pattern, shift the result by that difference. The ninety-minute cycle is also an average. Real cycles vary between about seventy and 110 minutes and change through the night, but ninety minutes is the standard figure used by sleep calculators and it gives a useful target.

    Bedtime = wake-up time - 15 minutes - (cycles x 90 minutes)
    Wake-up time = bedtime + 15 minutes + (cycles x 90 minutes)
    Example: to wake at 07:00 with six cycles, 07:00 - 15 minutes - 540 minutes = 21:45 the previous evening. With five cycles it is 23:15, and with four cycles it is 00:45. Going to bed at 23:00 gives wake-up options of 05:15 (four cycles), 06:45 (five cycles), and 08:15 (six cycles).

    Times that cross midnight are handled by wrapping around the 24-hour clock. A bedtime of 21:45 means the evening before your 07:00 alarm, not the same calendar day, and the calculator shows the clock time you need rather than a date. If you work night shifts, the same logic applies: enter the time you must wake, whatever hour that is, and the calculator will count backwards through your sleep window correctly.

    How much sleep do adults really need

    Most healthy adults need seven to nine hours of sleep per night, which is roughly five to six full cycles. Some adults function well on slightly less and others genuinely need more, and the right amount also changes with illness, heavy training, stress, and age. Teenagers need more than adults, typically eight to ten hours, while older adults often sleep a little less at night but may nap during the day. The number that matters is how you function: if you need an alarm and caffeine to get through the morning, fall asleep quickly whenever you sit still, or feel foggy most days, you are probably not getting enough, whatever the calculator says.

    Short sleep is not made up by one long lie-in at the weekend. Sleep debt can be partly repaid, but irregular bedtimes also confuse your body clock, which is why sleep experts stress regularity almost as much as duration. Going to bed and waking at similar times every day, including weekends as far as possible, helps your body expect sleep at the right time and makes falling asleep faster.

    Making the bedtime actually work

    A calculated bedtime only helps if you can fall asleep near it. Bright screens, caffeine late in the day, alcohol, and a hot or noisy bedroom all delay sleep or break it up, even if you are in bed on time. Caffeine can stay in your system for six hours or more, so an afternoon coffee can still be working at midnight. Alcohol may make you drowsy at first but it fragments sleep later in the night, which is why you can sleep eight hours after drinking and still wake up tired. A cool, dark, quiet room and a short wind-down routine, reading or stretching instead of scrolling, give the fifteen-minute falling-asleep allowance a realistic chance.

    1. Set a wind-down alarm

    Set an alarm thirty minutes before bedtime to stop work and screens. Without it, the bedtime you calculated is easy to miss by an hour without noticing.

    2. Keep the same wake time

    If you can only fix one time, fix wake-up time. A steady wake time anchors your body clock and makes bedtime naturally earlier over a few days.

    3. Do not watch the clock

    If you cannot sleep, lying there calculating the hours left usually makes it worse. Get up, do something calm in low light, and return when you feel sleepy.

    This calculator is an educational planning tool, not a medical device. If you snore loudly, stop breathing during sleep, regularly cannot fall asleep for weeks, fall asleep uncontrollably during the day, or feel exhausted despite enough time in bed, talk to a doctor. Those patterns can point to sleep apnoea, insomnia, or another sleep disorder that a bedtime calculator cannot diagnose or treat.

    Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

    Why 90-minute sleep cycles?
    Sleep runs in cycles of roughly 90 minutes, moving from light sleep to deep sleep and dreaming sleep. Waking at the end of a cycle, when sleep is lightest, usually feels easier than waking from deep sleep in the middle of a cycle.
    Why does the calculator add 15 minutes?
    Most people need some time to fall asleep after getting into bed. The calculator allows about 15 minutes for this, so the bedtime it shows is when to get into bed, with sleep starting roughly 15 minutes later.
    Is six hours of sleep enough?
    Four cycles, or six hours, is a short night and is not enough as a regular pattern for most adults, who need seven to nine hours. It is better than a half-finished cycle, but it should not become your normal target.
    What if I work night shifts?
    The same maths applies at any hour. Enter the time you must wake, even if it is in the afternoon or evening, and the calculator counts backwards through your sleep window, wrapping across midnight where needed.
    Is my data stored anywhere?
    No. This calculator runs entirely in your browser. Your times and mode choice are never sent to a server or stored by OmniTools.
