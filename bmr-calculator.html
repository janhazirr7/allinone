How to use the BMR and calorie calculator

Your basal metabolic rate, or BMR, is the number of calories your body burns at complete rest just to stay alive: breathing, keeping your heart beating, maintaining body temperature, and running your brain and organs. It is not the same as your total daily burn, because you also burn calories when you move, work, exercise, and digest food. This calculator first estimates your BMR, then multiplies it by an activity factor to estimate the calories you need each day to maintain your current weight.

  1. Choose metric or imperial unitsUse kilograms and centimetres, or pounds, feet, and inches, whichever you know. The calculator converts imperial values before it calculates anything.
  2. Enter your sex, age, height, and weightThese four values drive the Mifflin-St Jeor equation. Use your current weight and your height without shoes for the most accurate result.
  3. Pick an honest activity levelMost people overestimate how active they are. If you sit most of the day and exercise a few times a week, choose light or moderate, not very active.
  4. Read the three numbersYour BMR is the rest-only figure. The large headline number is your maintenance level. The two smaller figures show roughly 500 calories below and above maintenance, a common starting point for gradual weight loss or gain.

The Mifflin-St Jeor formula

Nutrition researchers widely use the Mifflin-St Jeor equation because it was validated against measured metabolic rates and tends to be more accurate for most adults than older formulas. It uses weight, height, age, and sex, because muscle mass, body size, and age all change how much energy the body uses at rest. Men typically have a slightly higher BMR than women of the same size and age, which is why the formula adds five calories for men and subtracts 161 for women. Those constants are part of the published equation, not a judgement about any individual person.

Men: BMR = 10 x weight (kg) + 6.25 x height (cm) - 5 x age + 5
Women: BMR = 10 x weight (kg) + 6.25 x height (cm) - 5 x age - 161
Example: a 30-year-old man who weighs 80 kg and is 180 cm tall has a BMR of 10 x 80 + 6.25 x 180 - 5 x 30 + 5 = 1,780 kcal per day at rest. At a moderate activity level, 1,780 x 1.55 = 2,759 kcal per day for maintenance.

No formula can measure your body directly. Real BMR varies with muscle mass, thyroid function, illness, pregnancy, and even room temperature. Treat the result as a solid starting estimate, then adjust it by watching your weight trend over two to four weeks. If your weight is steady, your maintenance estimate is close. If it drifts steadily in one direction, your true maintenance is higher or lower than the estimate by roughly that drift.

Activity levels and what they mean

The activity multiplier is where most calorie calculators go wrong, because a single word like active means different things to different people. The table below follows the standard Harris-Benedict style multipliers used with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, and it describes a whole week, not a single workout day.

LevelMultiplierTypical week
Sedentary1.2Desk job, little exercise, mostly sitting
Light1.375Light exercise or sport on one to three days
Moderate1.55Moderate exercise on three to five days
Active1.725Hard exercise on six to seven days
Very active1.9Physical job plus hard training, or twice-daily training

A useful rule is to start one level lower than you first want to pick, especially if your job is sedentary. You can always eat a little more if you are losing weight faster than planned, but starting too high and gaining weight you did not want is a slower problem to undo. Athletes, manual workers, and people with a lot of muscle mass may genuinely need the higher multipliers, and in that case the higher figure is the honest one.

How to use the result safely

Calories are only one part of health. The quality of your food, your sleep, your protein intake, and your strength training all matter for how you feel and for whether weight you lose comes from fat or from muscle. A deficit of about 500 calories a day typically leads to roughly half a kilogram of weight loss per week, which is a sustainable pace for most adults. Much larger deficits can leave you tired, hungry, and more likely to regain weight later, so they are best used only with professional guidance.

1. Track the trend, not one day

Weigh yourself a few times a week under the same conditions and look at the weekly average. Daily weight jumps up and down with water, food, and salt, so a single reading proves very little.

2. Keep protein and movement in the plan

When you eat fewer calories, enough protein and regular resistance exercise help protect muscle, so more of the weight you lose is fat. Walking every day also helps without making you extremely hungry.

3. Recalculate after a big change

As you lose or gain weight, your BMR changes too, because a smaller or larger body needs less or more energy. Re-run this calculator every five to ten kilograms of change, or every few months.

This calculator is an educational tool, not a medical device. It does not diagnose, treat, or replace advice from a doctor or dietitian. Children, teenagers, pregnant people, and anyone with an eating disorder, diabetes, thyroid disease, or another condition that affects metabolism should not use a generic calorie target without professional advice.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

What is BMR?
Basal metabolic rate (BMR) is the number of calories your body burns at rest to keep you alive, including breathing, circulation, and maintaining body temperature. It does not include calories burned through movement or exercise.
How is BMR different from TDEE?
BMR is your resting burn only, while total daily energy expenditure (TDEE) is your BMR multiplied by an activity factor, so it includes work, exercise, and everyday movement. This calculator shows both: BMR as the smaller figure and TDEE as your daily maintenance calories.
Why does the calculator ask for sex?
The Mifflin-St Jeor equation includes sex because, on average, men and women of the same height, weight, and age differ in muscle mass and body composition, which affects resting energy use. The formula adds 5 for men and subtracts 161 for women.
Can I use this calorie number to lose weight safely?
As a starting point, yes for most healthy adults: eating about 500 calories below maintenance usually leads to gradual weight loss of around half a kilogram per week. Much larger deficits, or any calorie target during illness or pregnancy, should be discussed with a health professional.
Is my data stored anywhere?
No. This calculator runs entirely in your browser. Your age, weight, height, and activity level are never sent to a server or stored by OmniTools.
