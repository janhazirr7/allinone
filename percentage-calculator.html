How to Use the OmniTools Percentage Calculator

Percentages appear everywhere in everyday life: shop discounts, exam scores, tax on a bill, salary raises, and interest on savings. This calculator handles the three questions people ask most often, all on one page, so you never have to guess which formula to use. Everything runs in your browser, and nothing you type is sent anywhere.

  1. Choose a calculation mode. Use "X% of Y" when you know a percent and a total, for example a 20% tip on a bill. Use "X is what % of Y" when you know a part and a whole, for example 45 marks out of 60. Use "Percentage change" when you compare an old value with a new one, for example a price that rose from $80 to $100.
  2. Enter the two numbers. Type plain numbers only, without a % sign or currency symbol. Decimals are fine, so you can enter 12.5 for twelve and a half percent, or 49.99 for a price.
  3. Click "Calculate". The result appears in the large card at the top of the results box, together with the exact formula used, so you can check the working at a glance. The smaller cards below it show useful extra details, such as how much is left over or the absolute difference between the two values.
  4. Copy or reset. Press "Copy Summary" to copy a plain-text summary you can paste into a message, homework answer, or spreadsheet note. Press "Reset" to clear all fields and start a fresh calculation.

The Three Percentage Formulas, Explained Simply

A percentage is just a fraction out of 100. The word comes from the Latin phrase "per centum", meaning "by the hundred", so 25% literally means 25 out of every 100. Once you remember that, every percentage problem becomes a simple fraction problem. Here are the three formulas this calculator uses, with nothing hidden:

Mode A: X% of Y = (X × Y) / 100
Mode B: X is what % of Y = (X / Y) × 100
Mode C: Percentage change = ((New − Old) / Old) × 100

In mode C, a positive answer means an increase and a negative answer means a decrease. If the new value equals the old value, the change is exactly 0%.

Notice that mode C always divides by the old value, not the new one. That is the most common mistake people make with percentage change: the starting point is the reference, so a rise from 50 to 75 is a 50% increase, because 25 divided by 50 is one half. Going back down from 75 to 50 is not a 50% decrease, it is a 33.33% decrease, because 25 divided by 75 is one third. Percentages are not symmetrical, and that asymmetry surprises almost everyone the first time they meet it.

Worked Examples You Can Try Right Now

Try these three examples in the calculator above and check that you get the same answers. Each one matches one of the three modes, and each one is the kind of problem that comes up in real life:

1. A tip on a restaurant bill

What is 20% of $85? Using mode A: (20 × 85) / 100 = 17. The tip is $17, so the total to pay is $102. The same idea works for a sales tax rate, a commission, or any discount expressed as a percent of a price.

2. An exam score as a percent

You scored 45 out of 60. What percent is that? Using mode B: (45 / 60) × 100 = 75%. Notice that the answer can be over 100% too: if you score 70 out of 60 with bonus marks, that is 116.67%, which simply means more than the full total.

3. A price rise, or a pay cut

A monthly bill rises from $80 to $100. Using mode C: ((100 − 80) / 80) × 100 = 25% increase. If instead a salary falls from $2,000 to $1,800, the same formula gives ((1,800 − 2,000) / 2,000) × 100 = −10%, a 10% decrease.

Tips and Common Mistakes to Avoid

First, never divide by zero: a percentage share of a zero total, or a change from a starting value of zero, has no meaningful answer, which is why this calculator shows a clear error message instead of a misleading number. Second, watch out for percentage points versus percent. If an interest rate rises from 4% to 6%, that is a rise of 2 percentage points, but a 50% relative increase, because 2 divided by 4 is one half. News reports mix these up often, so knowing the difference protects you from being misled. Third, remember that two equal-looking percent moves do not cancel out: a price that rises 10% and then falls 10% does not return to its starting value, because the fall is taken from a larger base. Finally, when a problem says "increase by 20%", the new value is 120% of the old one, not 20% of it. Add the percent to 100 before you multiply, or use mode C in reverse. Keeping these four habits in mind turns percentages from a source of confusion into a tool you can trust for shopping, studying, budgeting, and business decisions alike.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

What is a percentage?
A percentage is a way of expressing a number as a fraction of 100. The word comes from the Latin "per centum", meaning "by the hundred". So 25% simply means 25 out of every 100, or 25/100, which is the same as 0.25.
How do I calculate X% of Y?
Multiply X by Y and divide by 100: (X × Y) / 100. For example, 25% of 200 is (25 × 200) / 100 = 50.
How do I calculate a percentage increase or decrease?
Subtract the old value from the new value, divide by the old value, and multiply by 100: ((new − old) / old) × 100. A positive result is an increase and a negative result is a decrease. For example, going from 80 to 100 is ((100 − 80) / 80) × 100 = 25% increase.
What is the difference between percentage points and percent?
Percentage points measure the simple difference between two percentages, while percent measures the relative change. If a rate rises from 20% to 25%, that is a rise of 5 percentage points, but a 25% relative increase (5 / 20 × 100).
