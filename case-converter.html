How to Use the OmniTools Case Converter

The Six Case Styles, Explained

Each style follows one simple rule, applied to every letter in your text. Nothing else changes: your line breaks stay where they are, your spaces are not collapsed or trimmed, and numbers and punctuation are left exactly as you wrote them. The only thing that changes is the capitalisation of letters.

Example: “hello world. don't shout!”
UPPER CASE: HELLO WORLD. DON'T SHOUT!
lower case: hello world. don't shout!
Title Case: Hello World. Don't Shout!
Sentence case: Hello world. Don't shout!
aLtErNaTiNg case: hElLo WoRlD. dOn'T sHoUt! (spaces and punctuation do not flip the pattern)
iNVERSE case: HELLO WORLD. DON'T SHOUT! — every small letter becomes capital, and every capital becomes small.

UPPER CASE turns every letter into a capital. It is the loudest style, so use it sparingly. Lower case does the opposite: every letter becomes small, which is useful when you have pasted text that arrived in all capitals, for example from a scanned form, a spreadsheet header, or a shouting email thread you want to calm down before replying.

Title Case capitalises the first letter of each word and lowers the rest. It treats an apostrophe as part of the word, so “don't” becomes “Don't” and “can't” becomes “Can't”, rather than the common mistake “Don'T”. Hyphenated words are treated word by word, so “well-known” becomes “Well-Known”. Sentence case is different: it lowers the whole text first, then capitalises only the first letter of the text and the first letter after each full stop, exclamation mark, or question mark that is followed by a space. Everything else in the sentence stays lower case, which is exactly how ordinary sentences are written.

Alternating case alternates lower and upper letter by letter, starting with a lower-case first letter: “hello world” becomes “hElLo WoRlD”. Spaces, punctuation, and numbers do not advance the pattern and do not flip it, so the rhythm stays tied to letters only — the space after “hello” does not make the “w” of “world” start a new pattern. Inverse case simply swaps every letter: capitals become small and small letters become capitals, so “Hello” becomes “hELLO”. It is the fastest way to fix text typed with Caps Lock on, or text pasted in the opposite case to the one you wanted.

When to Use Each Style: Titles, Sentences, Code and Social Media

The right style depends on where the text is going. For titles — blog posts, video titles, presentation slides, book chapters, and headings — Title Case is the standard choice in English, because it looks polished and is easy to scan. For ordinary writing, such as paragraphs, emails, essays, and messages, Sentence case is correct: it reads naturally and never looks like shouting. Reserve UPPER CASE for very short labels where emphasis is genuinely needed, such as a warning, a section divider, or a single-word heading, and avoid whole paragraphs in capitals, which are harder to read and can come across as aggressive online.

1. Titles and Headings

Use Title Case for article titles, slide headings, and product names, for example “How To Brew Better Coffee At Home”. It gives a finished, professional look and matches what readers expect from a headline.

2. Sentences and Paragraphs

Use Sentence case for body text, emails, and essays. Paste a draft that arrived in all capitals, convert it once, and you have clean, readable prose without retyping a single sentence.

3. Code, Forms and Social Media

Use lower case when a form, username field, or piece of code expects it, and use UPPER CASE for short labels or acronyms. Alternating and inverse case are playful styles best kept for memes, jokes, and casual social posts, not for formal documents.

A practical habit is to convert first and proofread second. Case conversion is mechanical, but meaning is not: a name, a brand, or an acronym such as “NASA” or “iPhone” may need its original capitalisation restored by hand after conversion, because no automatic rule knows that “nasa” in your sentence is an acronym. The same applies to sentence case and abbreviations. The rule here is simple and stated openly: a sentence is treated as ending at a full stop, exclamation mark, or question mark followed by a space. That handles ordinary writing perfectly, but abbreviations such as “e.g.”, “Dr.”, or “Mr.” may be treated as sentence ends, so the next word may be capitalised when you did not intend it. Give the converted text one quick read, fix any abbreviation that was split, and then copy it. That single check is far faster than retyping the whole passage.

Rules, Edge Cases and Privacy

Three rules cover almost every surprise. First, whitespace is sacred: multiple spaces, tabs, blank lines, and line breaks pass through untouched, so a poem, a list, or a carefully indented passage keeps its shape. Second, only letters change case. Digits such as “2026”, symbols such as “@” and “#”, and punctuation such as commas and brackets are copied unchanged, which means phone numbers, prices, and code snippets survive conversion intact. Third, the conversion is reversible in most cases but not all: converting “Hello World” to lower case and back to Title Case restores it, but converting “iPhone” to lower case loses the capital “P” forever, because the tool cannot know it was there. Keep your original text until you are happy with the result, or convert a copy.

Privacy is equally straightforward. This tool runs entirely in your browser with plain client-side code and no account, no upload, and no server round trip. Your text never leaves your device, which means you can safely convert private drafts, work documents, and personal messages. When you click “Copy Result”, only your own clipboard is used, and when you click “Clear”, both boxes are emptied at once. Nothing is stored, nothing is tracked, and nothing is shared — the conversion exists only on the page in front of you, for as long as you keep it open.
