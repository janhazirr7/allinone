How to use the pregnancy calculator

Pregnancy is dated from the first day of your last menstrual period, even though conception usually happens about two weeks later. That convention can feel strange at first, but it exists because the start of a period is a date most people can remember, while the exact day of conception usually cannot be known without fertility tracking. Doctors, midwives, and ultrasound reports all use this same dating, so the week number this calculator gives you will match what you hear at appointments, provided your cycle is roughly regular.

  1. Enter the first day of your last periodUse the first day of bleeding, not the last day and not the day you think conception happened. If you are unsure by a few days, the result will be uncertain by the same few days.
  2. Leave today's date blank for a current resultThe calculator uses the real today when you leave the second field empty. Fill it in only if you want to check what week you were on a past date.
  3. Read the due date firstThe large headline is your estimated due date, 280 days after your last period. It is an estimate for planning, not a guaranteed delivery day.
  4. Read your week and trimesterYour current week is shown as weeks plus extra days, for example week 12 plus 3 days. The trimester tells you which third of pregnancy you are in, which is how care and screening are organised.

How the calculation works

The standard rule, known as Naegele's rule, adds 280 days, or forty weeks, to the first day of the last menstrual period. That is the same as adding one year, subtracting three months, and adding seven days, which is how the rule was traditionally taught before calculators existed. Both forms give the same date when applied correctly. Forty weeks is divided into three trimesters: the first trimester runs from week 0 through week 13, the second from week 14 through week 27, and the third from week 28 until birth. A pregnancy is considered full term from 39 weeks, and most babies arrive within about two weeks either side of the due date.

Due date = first day of last period + 280 days (40 weeks)
Example: last period starting on 1 January 2026 gives a due date of 8 October 2026. On 1 April 2026, that pregnancy is 90 days along, which is 12 weeks and 6 days, in the first trimester, with 27 weeks and 1 day remaining.

This method assumes a regular cycle of about 28 days with ovulation around day 14. If your cycles are much longer or shorter, or irregular, the date from your last period can be off by days or even weeks. That is why an early ultrasound, ideally in the first trimester, is the more accurate dating method: it measures the baby directly rather than assuming when ovulation happened. If your ultrasound date differs from your last-period date, your care team will usually use the ultrasound date from then on.

What each trimester means in practice

The first trimester is when the baby's organs form, which is why folic acid before and during early pregnancy matters so much, and why many people feel nausea, tiredness, and breast tenderness in these weeks. It is also when the first screening tests and the first ultrasound usually happen. The second trimester is often the most comfortable period: nausea usually eases, energy returns, and the baby's movements become noticeable, typically between weeks 18 and 22 for a first pregnancy. The third trimester brings faster growth, more frequent appointments, and practical preparation, while sleep often becomes harder as the baby grows larger.

These are averages, not rules for any individual pregnancy. Some people feel well in the first trimester and unwell later, and some babies move earlier or later than the typical range. What matters is the pattern for you and what your care team observes at appointments, not whether your experience matches a table exactly.

Limits, safety, and when to seek care

This calculator gives an estimate, not a diagnosis and not a substitute for prenatal care. It cannot tell you whether a pregnancy is viable, whether there are twins, or whether development is on track; only examination and ultrasound can do that. If you have a positive pregnancy test, book prenatal care promptly, even if the due date seems far away, because early folic acid, blood tests, and screening have time windows. If you are unsure of your last period date, tell your care team rather than guessing and treating the calculator result as exact.

1. Book care early

Contact a doctor or midwife as soon as you have a positive test. Early care is about screening and support, not just the due date.

2. Trust the ultrasound date

If an early scan gives a different date from your last period, use the scan date. It measures the baby instead of assuming your cycle length.

3. Seek care urgently for warning signs

Heavy bleeding, severe pain, leaking fluid, fever, severe headache, vision changes, or a sudden change in baby movements all need prompt medical attention, at any week.

Only about one in twenty babies arrives exactly on the due date, so treat it as the middle of a window rather than an appointment. Babies born between 37 and 42 weeks are generally considered term, with 39 to 40 weeks regarded as full term. If you pass 41 weeks, your care team will discuss monitoring and options for induction. This calculator is for education and planning only; for any medical question about your pregnancy, talk to your doctor or midwife.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

How is a due date calculated?
A due date is estimated by adding 280 days, or 40 weeks, to the first day of your last menstrual period. This method, called Naegele's rule, assumes a regular 28-day cycle with ovulation around day 14.
Why is pregnancy dated from my last period, not conception?
Conception usually happens about two weeks after the last period starts, but the exact day is often unknown. The first day of the last period is easier to remember and is the standard starting point used by doctors and ultrasound reports.
What if my cycles are irregular?
If your cycles are much longer, shorter, or irregular than 28 days, a last-period date can be off by days or weeks. An early ultrasound, which measures the baby directly, is more accurate in that case and is usually used instead.
Does only one in twenty babies arrive on the due date?
Yes, roughly. Most babies arrive within about two weeks either side of the due date, and births between 37 and 42 weeks are generally considered term, with 39 to 40 weeks regarded as full term.
Is my data stored anywhere?
No. This calculator runs entirely in your browser. Your dates are never sent to a server or stored by OmniTools.
